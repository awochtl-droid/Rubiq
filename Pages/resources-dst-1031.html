DST 1031 Exchange: How a Delaware Statutory Trust Works
Rules, Properties, Pros, Cons & Risks

Updated October 2026 9 min read Andreas Wochtl, CFP® · Rubiq Financial Partners

A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) is a trust that owns investment real estate and sells fractional shares of it to investors. Because the IRS treats a qualifying DST interest as ownership of real estate, investors can buy one as replacement property in a 1031 exchange and defer the tax on their gain. In return they give up control and liquidity: a sponsor runs the property, and the money is typically tied up for five to ten years.

DST pros

  • Passive: no tenants, repairs, or management
  • Can qualify as 1031 replacement property
  • Access to larger, institutional-quality buildings
  • Split proceeds across several properties
  • Loan already in place can help replace debt and close on time

DST cons

  • Illiquid, typically for 5 to 10 years
  • No say in operations or when the property sells
  • Upfront and ongoing fees reduce returns
  • Distributions are not guaranteed; sponsor risk
  • IRS rules bar refinancing, new capital, or new leases

At a glance

A qualifying deferred exchange generally has a 45-day identification period and must be completed by the earlier of 180 days after the sale or the due date of the tax return (including extensions). Tax deferral depends on meeting the exchange rules; DST ownership also brings illiquidity, limited control, sponsor, property, and fee risks.

What Is a Delaware Statutory Trust?

A Delaware Statutory Trust (DST) is a legal entity created under Delaware law that holds title to one or more investment properties. Unlike traditional real estate partnerships or direct ownership, investors in a DST purchase beneficial interests in the trust rather than taking direct title to the underlying property. Each investor's fractional interest, however, is treated as direct ownership of real estate for federal tax purposes — a distinction that makes the structure uniquely powerful.

IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86 describes circumstances in which a beneficial interest in a DST may be treated as an interest in real property for Section 1031 purposes. The trust must satisfy the ruling's requirements, and the transaction must independently meet applicable exchange rules. A DST interest is not automatically eligible simply because it is labeled a DST.

The trust is managed by a sponsor under the terms of the trust and offering documents. Investors generally do not manage the property or make day-to-day operating decisions, and distributions, if any, depend on property performance and are not guaranteed.

How a DST 1031 Exchange Works

The mechanics of using a DST within a 1031 exchange follow the same fundamental timeline as other deferred exchanges. A DST may provide a passive replacement-property option, but does not remove the exchange's timing, identification, or tax requirements.

In a typical deferred exchange, a qualified intermediary (QI) is engaged before the sale closes to facilitate the exchange and prevent the taxpayer from receiving or controlling the proceeds. Replacement property generally must be identified in writing within 45 days after the transfer. The exchange must be completed by the earlier of 180 days after transfer or the due date of the taxpayer's return for that year, including extensions. Specific rules and exceptions can apply, so confirm the deadline with a qualified tax professional.

Fractional DST interests may give an exchanger access to replacement property without acquiring an entire building. Investors may identify more than one replacement property, subject to the exchange identification rules, and may allocate among offerings if they meet the applicable requirements. That flexibility does not guarantee an exact match, diversification, or full tax deferral; value, equity, debt, cash received, and other transaction details matter.

Under common identification rules, an exchanger may identify up to three replacement properties regardless of value (the "three-property rule") or identify more properties whose total value does not exceed 200% of the relinquished property's value (the "200% rule"). A 95% exception may apply when those limits are exceeded, but its requirements are stringent. How a DST offering is identified can depend on the transaction and offering documents; confirm the identification with the qualified intermediary and tax counsel.

Adjusted Basis and a DST 1031 Exchange Estimate

Adjusted tax basis is one input used to estimate gain when investment real estate is sold. As a simplified starting point, adjusted basis is generally the property's tax basis reduced by depreciation allowed or allowable; other tax-basis adjustments may also apply. The amount realized, selling costs, debt, and transaction details can affect the calculation, so purchase price alone is not enough to determine tax.

Our DST 1031 exchange calculator asks for tax basis before depreciation and accumulated depreciation separately, then uses the entered property and tax assumptions to illustrate a possible sell-versus-exchange scenario. It is not a tax calculation or a determination that a DST or exchange qualifies. Have a tax professional verify adjusted basis, gain, and exchange treatment for your situation.

DST 1031 Properties: What You Can Buy

DST sponsors package institutional-quality real estate into offerings sold through broker-dealers and advisors. Common property types include:

  • Multifamily. Apartment communities, including student and senior housing. Leases are short, so income can adjust quickly in either direction.
  • Industrial and distribution. Warehouses and logistics buildings, often leased to a single tenant.
  • Net-lease retail. Pharmacies, grocery stores, and other single-tenant buildings where the tenant pays most expenses. Income depends heavily on that tenant’s credit.
  • Medical office and self-storage. Niche property types with their own demand drivers and risks.
  • Portfolios. Some DSTs hold several properties, which spreads tenant and location risk across one investment.

Who can invest. DST interests are private securities offerings, generally limited to accredited investors: broadly, a net worth above $1 million excluding your home, or income above $200,000 ($300,000 with a spouse) in each of the last two years. Each offering sets its own minimum, often around $100,000 for 1031 investors.

Timing. Offerings close once they are fully subscribed, so an interest available on day 10 of your 45-day window may be gone by day 40. Investors often line up a first choice and a backup before the sale closes, within the identification rules above.

Replacing debt. To defer all of the gain, the replacement generally needs to be worth at least as much as what you sold, including any mortgage paid off at closing. A DST that already carries financing can help replace that debt without a new personal loan, while debt-free DSTs suit sellers with little or no mortgage.

State taxes: Pennsylvania and California

Pennsylvania did not recognize 1031 exchanges for personal income tax until Act 53 of 2022. For exchanges from tax year 2023 onward, Pennsylvania generally follows the federal deferral, so a qualifying DST exchange can defer state tax as well. See our Pennsylvania retirement tax guide for how the state treats other income.

California tracks gain deferred when California property is exchanged into property elsewhere, including an out-of-state DST. The investor files Form FTB 3840 each year until that gain is recognized, and California can tax it when the replacement property is eventually sold in a taxable sale.

Benefits for Real Estate Investors

The primary appeal of DSTs lies in the convergence of several benefits that are difficult to achieve simultaneously through other real estate structures:

Potential tax deferral. A qualifying Section 1031 exchange may defer recognition of some or all gain, depending on the facts and whether all requirements are met. The amount of any tax due on a sale depends on the investor's basis, depreciation, applicable tax rules, and other circumstances; the DST structure does not guarantee deferral or a particular tax outcome.

Passive management. DST investors generally do not manage the property or make day-to-day operating decisions. Investors give up direct control and remain exposed to the sponsor's decisions and the property's performance.

Access to pooled real estate. Some DST offerings hold larger commercial properties or portfolios managed by a sponsor. Property type, tenant quality, operations, fees, leverage, and investment terms vary by offering and should be assessed in its offering documents.

Estate planning considerations. The treatment of an inherited DST interest and any deferred gain depends on the law in effect at the time and the investor's circumstances. Do not assume that a future basis adjustment will eliminate deferred tax; consult estate and tax professionals about your situation.

Risks and Limitations

DSTs are not without significant drawbacks, and any investor considering this strategy should understand the full risk profile before proceeding:

  • Illiquidity. DST interests are not publicly traded and typically require a 5- to 10-year hold period until the sponsor disposes of the underlying property. There is no established secondary market, and early exits — when available at all — often come at a steep discount to net asset value. Investors must be prepared to have their capital locked up for the full duration of the trust.
  • No guarantee of distributions. While DSTs typically project monthly or quarterly income distributions, these are not guaranteed. Rental income depends on tenant occupancy, lease rates, and property performance. Distributions can be reduced or suspended entirely if the property underperforms or faces unexpected capital needs.
  • Sponsor risk. The quality of the DST investment is heavily dependent on the sponsor's competence, integrity, and financial stability. A poorly managed DST with an inexperienced or undercapitalized sponsor can result in reduced distributions, deferred maintenance, and ultimately a loss of principal at disposition.
  • The "seven deadly sins." IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86 imposes strict operational restrictions on DSTs, often referred to as the "seven deadly sins." The trustee may not: (1) accept new capital contributions once the offering closes, (2) renegotiate existing loans or borrow new money, (3) reinvest proceeds from the sale of its real estate, (4) make capital expenditures beyond normal repair and maintenance, minor non-structural improvements, and those required by law, (5) invest cash held between distributions in anything other than short-term debt obligations, (6) hold back cash rather than distributing it currently, other than reasonable reserves, or (7) enter into new leases or renegotiate existing ones, except in limited situations such as a tenant's bankruptcy or insolvency. These restrictions limit the sponsor's ability to respond to changing market conditions and can create vulnerability if the property requires significant capital investment.
  • Meaningful fees. DST offerings typically carry upfront fees (often 10–15% of the invested amount, including selling commissions, offering and organizational costs, and acquisition fees), ongoing asset management fees, and disposition fees. These costs reduce the investor's effective yield and total return compared to direct property ownership.
  • No active management control. Investors have no vote, no ability to influence operational decisions, and no recourse if they disagree with the sponsor's management approach. This passive structure, while a benefit for those seeking to exit active management, can be frustrating for experienced real estate investors accustomed to controlling their own assets.

Some DSTs are designed to roll into the sponsor’s REIT through a 721 exchange, which ends future 1031 flexibility. And because a 1031 defers rather than erases depreciation recapture, it is worth knowing how much recapture you are carrying forward.

Due Diligence: How to Evaluate DST 1031 Offerings

Not all DST offerings are created equal. The difference between a well-structured DST from a reputable sponsor and a poorly underwritten offering from an inexperienced one can be the difference between a successful retirement income stream and a significant loss of capital. Here is what we evaluate:

  • Sponsor track record. How many DST offerings has the sponsor completed? What were the actual returns versus projected returns on prior programs? How did their properties perform during economic downturns? A sponsor with 20+ completed full-cycle DST programs and a track record of meeting or exceeding projections is in a fundamentally different category than a sponsor on their second or third offering.
  • Property quality and location. The underlying real estate must justify the investment on its own merits, independent of the tax benefits. We evaluate tenant quality, lease terms, market fundamentals, comparable sales data, and long-term demand drivers for the property type and location.
  • Leverage ratio. The amount of debt on the property relative to its value is one of the most important risk factors. Lower leverage (50% or below loan-to-value) generally means less risk of a cash flow shortfall or loss of principal if property values decline. Higher leverage amplifies both returns and losses.
  • Projected vs. historical returns. We compare the projected cash-on-cash yield and total return against the sponsor's historical performance across similar property types. Projected returns that significantly exceed the sponsor's track record should be viewed skeptically.
  • Fee transparency. We want to see a clear breakdown of all fees — upfront, ongoing, and at disposition. Sponsors who bury fees in complex structures or use opaque language about compensation are less likely to be aligned with investor interests.
  • Distribution coverage ratio. This metric shows whether the property's net operating income is sufficient to cover the projected distributions with a margin of safety. A coverage ratio below 1.0x means the sponsor is paying distributions from reserves or return of capital — a red flag for sustainability.

How Rubiq Approaches DSTs

We evaluate DST offerings from multiple sponsors and compare them against alternative strategies — direct replacement property, TIC (tenants-in-common) arrangements, installment sales, and in some cases simply paying the tax and reinvesting the net proceeds. The 1031 exchange creates urgency, but urgency should not override sound investment analysis.

For clients who want to remain in real estate but exit active management, we model an illustrative after-tax income comparison between a DST portfolio and selling outright. The analysis considers potential deferred tax, projected (not guaranteed) DST distributions, the opportunity cost of capital locked in an illiquid investment, and estate and basis treatment under current law. Results vary by the client's circumstances, and a DST does not always produce a better outcome.

We never recommend a DST simply to defer taxes — the underlying real estate and sponsor quality must justify the investment independently. A DST with a subpar property or a questionable sponsor is a poor investment regardless of the tax deferral it provides. Our role is to ensure that clients make informed decisions based on the full picture: the tax benefits, the investment quality, the risks, the fees, and the alternatives.

Common DST and 1031 Exchange Questions

What is a Delaware Statutory Trust (DST)?

A Delaware Statutory Trust is a trust formed under Delaware law that owns investment real estate, such as apartment buildings, warehouses, or net-leased retail, and sells fractional beneficial interests to investors. A sponsor manages the property, and investors receive their share of net income and depreciation. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, an interest in a qualifying DST is treated as a direct interest in real estate, so it can be used as replacement property in a 1031 exchange.

What are the pros and cons of a DST?

Potential advantages include passive ownership with no landlord duties, eligibility as 1031 replacement property, access to larger institutional-quality properties, the ability to split proceeds across several DSTs, and existing financing that can help replace debt. Disadvantages include illiquidity for typically 5 to 10 years, no investor control over operations or sale timing, upfront and ongoing fees, sponsor risk, distributions that are not guaranteed, and IRS restrictions that prevent the trust from refinancing, raising new capital, or entering new leases.

What is a DST 1031 exchange?

A Delaware Statutory Trust is a legal entity that may hold investment real estate for multiple beneficial owners. Under IRS Revenue Ruling 2004-86, an interest in certain DSTs may qualify as replacement real property for a Section 1031 exchange when the trust and transaction meet applicable requirements. Eligibility is not automatic.

Who can invest in a DST 1031 property?

DST interests are private securities offerings generally limited to accredited investors, such as individuals with a net worth over $1 million excluding their primary residence, or income over $200,000 ($300,000 jointly) in each of the last two years. Each offering sets its own minimum investment, often around $100,000 for 1031 exchange investors.

What are the 45-day and 180-day deadlines?

Replacement property generally must be identified in writing within 45 days after the relinquished property is transferred. The exchange generally must be completed by the earlier of 180 days after transfer or the due date of the taxpayer's return, including extensions. A qualified intermediary and strict procedures are generally required; ask a qualified tax professional about the rules that apply to your transaction.

Does a 1031 exchange eliminate capital gains tax?

A qualifying Section 1031 exchange generally defers recognition of gain rather than eliminating tax. Cash or other non-like-kind property received, liabilities not replaced, or other circumstances may result in taxable gain. Future tax treatment depends on applicable law and the taxpayer's situation.

How does adjusted basis affect a DST 1031 exchange estimate?

Adjusted tax basis generally starts with a property's tax basis and is reduced by depreciation allowed or allowable, with other adjustments possible under tax rules. The calculator asks for basis before depreciation and accumulated depreciation separately to create a simplified illustration; actual basis and taxable gain depend on transaction details and individual circumstances. Confirm them with a qualified tax professional.

What are the risks of investing in a DST?

DST investments can be illiquid and offer limited investor control. Investors face property, sponsor, leverage, financing, tenant, and market risks; projected returns and distributions are not guaranteed, and fees and costs can reduce returns. Review offering documents, sponsor information, property fundamentals, debt terms, conflicts, and exit assumptions with qualified professionals.

Are DST distributions or returns guaranteed?

No. Distributions and returns are not guaranteed and may change or stop. Investors can lose some or all of their principal, and may not be able to sell their DST interest when they want. Review the offering documents and risks before investing.
