Buy-Sell Agreements and Key Person Insurance: A Guide for Business Owners

If a partner dies, becomes disabled or wants out, the buy-sell agreement decides what happens to their share and what their family is paid. Here is how these agreements work, how they are funded, what a 2024 Supreme Court ruling changed, and where key person insurance fits.

The short answer

A buy-sell agreement is a contract among owners that says who can buy a departing owner’s share, what triggers the sale, and how the price is set. It only works if there is money to pay for it, which is why most are funded with life and disability insurance. Key person insurance is different: it pays the company for the loss of someone it depends on, and it does not transfer ownership.

What a buy-sell agreement covers

  • Triggers. Death, disability, retirement, divorce, bankruptcy, and leaving the company, each with its own terms.
  • Who buys. The company, the other owners, or a choice between them.
  • The price. A fixed number updated on a schedule, a formula, or an independent appraisal at the time.
  • Payment terms. A lump sum from insurance, installments over several years, or both.

Without one, a deceased owner’s family can end up as an unplanned business partner with no say in pay or distributions, and the surviving owners can end up negotiating a price with a grieving spouse.

The Connelly ruling changed entity-purchase plans

In June 2024 the U.S. Supreme Court decided Connelly v. United States. Two brothers owned a company that held life insurance to redeem either brother’s shares at death. The Court held that the insurance proceeds increase the value of the company for federal estate tax, and that the company’s obligation to buy the shares does not offset them.

The effect: a deceased owner’s estate can be taxed on a value inflated by the very insurance meant to buy it out. Owners whose estates could exceed the federal exemption of $15.48 million per person in 2027 should have an entity-purchase agreement reviewed. Common responses are a cross-purchase structure or a separate insurance entity. That is a decision for your attorney.

Setting the price

  • Fixed price. Easy to understand and almost always out of date. If you use one, require an annual update and a fallback when it lapses.
  • Formula. A multiple of earnings or revenue. It updates itself, but can produce odd results in an unusual year.
  • Appraisal. An independent valuation at the time of the trigger. Most accurate, slower, and it costs money.

Whatever the method, the insurance should be re-sized when the value changes. A business that has tripled in value with coverage set ten years ago leaves the buyers borrowing the difference.

Funding the buyout

  • Life insurance for a death. Term coverage is common when the need ends at retirement; permanent coverage when it does not.
  • Disability buyout insurance for a long-term disability, which is more likely than death during working years and is often left unfunded.
  • Installment payments from company cash flow for retirement or a voluntary exit, where insurance does not apply.
  • Savings set aside in the company or by the owners.

Key person insurance

The company buys a policy on a person whose loss would damage the business, pays the premiums, and is the beneficiary. The proceeds can cover lost profits, reassure lenders and customers, and pay to recruit a replacement. Coverage is usually sized to a multiple of the person’s pay or their contribution to profit, or to a specific loan.

The tax rules that catch owners out

  • Premiums are generally not deductible when the company is the beneficiary.
  • Notice and consent come first. For company-owned policies issued after August 17, 2006, the insured must be notified in writing and consent in writing before the policy is issued. If that step is missed, the death benefit above the premiums paid can be taxable to the company.
  • Form 8925 must be filed with the company’s tax return each year the policy is in force.

These rules also apply to company-owned policies that fund an entity-purchase buy-sell agreement.

When to review the agreement

  • The business value has changed materially.
  • An owner joins, leaves, marries or divorces.
  • The company changes its tax structure.
  • An owner’s estate approaches the federal exemption.
  • It has been more than three years.

Frequently asked questions

What is a buy-sell agreement?

A buy-sell agreement is a contract among the owners of a business that sets out what happens to an owner’s share after a triggering event such as death, disability, retirement or divorce. It states who may buy the share, how the price is set, and how it will be paid.

What is the difference between a cross-purchase and an entity-purchase agreement?

In a cross-purchase agreement the remaining owners buy the departing owner’s share, and each owner typically owns insurance on the others. In an entity-purchase or redemption agreement the company buys the share and owns the insurance. A cross-purchase gives the buyers a higher tax basis but needs more policies.

How did Connelly v. United States affect buy-sell agreements?

In June 2024 the Supreme Court held that life insurance proceeds received by a company to redeem a deceased owner’s shares increase the company’s value for federal estate tax, and that the obligation to redeem the shares does not offset them. Entity-purchase agreements funded with company-owned insurance should be reviewed.

What is key person insurance?

Key person insurance is life or disability insurance that a business buys on an owner or employee it depends on. The business pays the premiums and receives the benefit, which can cover lost profits, loan obligations, and the cost of finding a replacement. It does not transfer ownership.

Are key person insurance premiums tax-deductible?

Generally no, when the business is the beneficiary. The death benefit is generally received free of income tax if the notice and consent requirements for employer-owned life insurance were met before the policy was issued and Form 8925 is filed each year.

How often should a buy-sell agreement be reviewed?

At least every three years, and whenever the business value changes materially, an owner joins or leaves, an owner marries or divorces, or the company changes its tax structure. The insurance funding should be re-sized at the same time.
