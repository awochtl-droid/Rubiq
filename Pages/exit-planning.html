Exit Planning for Business Owners: Timeline, Taxes and What You Keep

Most owners sell a business once. The price is negotiated in months, but how much of it you keep is decided by planning that starts years earlier. Here is how an exit plan is built, what a sale leaves after tax, and who does what.

The short answer

Exit planning is the work of deciding when, how and to whom you will hand over your business, and making sure what you receive is enough to fund the rest of your life. It usually takes three to five years, because the steps that add the most value need time: cleaning up the financials, reducing the company’s dependence on you, and setting up the tax planning before a buyer appears.

Ways to exitWho buysTypical trade-off
Sale to a third partyA competitor, a larger company or a private equity firmUsually the highest price; least control over what happens next
Management buyoutYour leadership teamContinuity; often paid over time from company cash flow
Family transferChildren or other relativesKeeps the business in the family; price and fairness to other heirs need care
Employee stock ownership plan (ESOP)A trust for your employeesTax advantages and legacy; complex and costly to set up

The three numbers that drive an exit plan

  • What the business is worth. A realistic range from a valuation, not a guess or a multiple heard from a friend.
  • What you keep. The price less taxes, transaction fees and any debt paid off at closing.
  • What you need. The amount of invested capital that supports your spending after the paycheck and the perks stop.

The difference between what you keep and what you need is the gap the plan has to close, by growing the business, saving more outside it, changing the deal structure, or adjusting the timeline.

A worked example

A Pennsylvania owner expects to sell her company for $8 million in a stock sale. Her tax basis is $1 million, so the gain is $7 million.

Amount
Sale price$8,000,000
Federal long-term capital gains tax (20% of $7,000,000)−$1,400,000
Pennsylvania income tax (3.07% of $7,000,000)−$214,900
Proceeds after income tax$6,385,100

She keeps about 80% of the price before transaction fees and any debt. If she wants $300,000 a year from the proceeds, that is about 4.7% of $6.4 million each year, a rate many retirees would find hard to sustain for 30 years. Knowing that three years ahead gives her time to respond. Knowing it at closing does not.

Hypothetical and simplified for illustration. It assumes all gain is long-term capital gain, ignores the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (which may apply depending on how active the owner is), transaction fees, debt, and any QSBS exclusion, and is not a projection of investment results. Actual results depend on your facts.

A timeline that works

  1. Three to five years out. Get a valuation. Build a management team that can run the company without you. Start the tax planning that needs time, such as large retirement plan contributions and gifts of shares to family or trusts while the value is lower.
  2. One to two years out. Have your CPA prepare the financials the way a buyer will read them. Review contracts, leases and your buy-sell agreement. Choose an investment banker or broker.
  3. During the sale. Compare offers on what they leave you after tax, not on headline price. Decide how much to take in cash, an earn-out, a seller note or rolled equity.
  4. After closing. Invest the proceeds, replace your income, and update your estate plan for assets that are now liquid.

How deal structure changes what you keep

  • Asset sale or stock sale. Buyers often prefer to buy assets; sellers usually prefer to sell stock. The allocation of the price can turn capital gain into ordinary income. Our business planning page shows a $170,000 difference on a $5 million sale, and depreciation recapture explains why.
  • Qualified small business stock. Owners of qualifying C corporation stock may be able to exclude a large part of the gain from federal tax. Eligibility depends on decisions made years earlier. See the QSBS guide.
  • Installment sale. Taking part of the price over several years spreads the gain, and it also makes you a lender to the buyer.
  • Earn-out. Part of the price depends on future results you may no longer control.
  • Rolled equity. Keeping a stake in the buyer can defer tax on that portion and gives you a second sale later, with the risk that goes with it.
  • Charitable planning. Gifts of shares to a donor-advised fund or charitable trust generally have to be completed before a sale is binding to be effective.

Pennsylvania owners

Pennsylvania taxes the gain on a sale at a flat 3.07% and does not follow every federal rule, including the federal QSBS exclusion. After the sale, what you leave to children is subject to the 4.5% Pennsylvania inheritance tax, which is one reason to consider lifetime gifts before a transaction.

Frequently asked questions

What is exit planning?

Exit planning is the process of preparing a business owner and the business for a change in ownership. It covers how and to whom the business will be transferred, what the owner needs from the sale, how the transaction will be taxed, and how the proceeds will be invested afterward.

When should I start exit planning?

Three to five years before you expect to sell is a common guideline. Building a management team, producing clean financial statements, and tax strategies such as retirement plan contributions or gifts of shares all take several years to pay off.

What are the ways to exit a business?

The main routes are a sale to a third party such as a competitor or private equity firm, a management buyout, a transfer to family members, and a sale to an employee stock ownership plan. Each differs in price, tax treatment, how you are paid, and how much control you keep.

How is the sale of a business taxed?

It depends on the structure. In a stock sale, most of the gain is usually taxed as long-term capital gain, at a top federal rate of 20%, and the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax may apply. In an asset sale, part of the price can be taxed as ordinary income. Pennsylvania taxes the gain at 3.07%.

What is the difference between exit planning and succession planning?

Succession planning is about who will lead and own the business next. Exit planning is broader: it adds the owner’s personal finances, the tax on the transaction, and what happens to the proceeds. A family or management succession is one kind of exit.

Does a financial advisor sell my business for me?

No. An investment banker or business broker runs the sale. A financial advisor sets the amount you need from the sale, coordinates the tax and estate planning with your CPA and attorney, and invests the proceeds after closing.

Before you talk to a buyer

Exit readiness check

  • You have a valuation less than two years old.
  • The business can run for a month without you.
  • Three years of financials are clean and reviewed.
  • You know your after-tax number and what you need.
  • Your buy-sell agreement and estate plan are current.
  • You have picked your attorney, CPA and banker.
