Liquidity Planning

UHNW Lending & Liquidity: Ways to Access Capital Without Losing Sight of the Plan

A major purchase, tax bill, business opportunity, or uneven cash flow can create a need for capital. The right liquidity strategy depends on timing, assets, borrowing costs, taxes, and the risks you can tolerate.

Liquidity is more than cash in a bank account. For an ultra-high-net-worth household, it is the ability to meet a need on time without creating avoidable tax costs, selling assets at an unfavorable moment, or taking on debt that strains the rest of the financial plan.

Start with the need, not the loan

Before comparing borrowing options, define how much is needed, when it is needed, how long it will be outstanding, and what will repay it. A short-term bridge for a known cash event is different from recurring spending funded by debt. Map the need against household cash flows, planned asset sales, business distributions, upcoming tax payments, and existing obligations.

Then consider the whole balance sheet. Assets that appear available may be restricted, pledged elsewhere, held in retirement accounts, concentrated in one security, or needed for near-term spending. A source of funds should be evaluated alongside its costs and the consequences if assumptions change.

Common ways to access liquidity

Option 1

Cash and short-term reserves

Cash, Treasury bills, and other short-duration reserves can cover planned spending without borrowing or selling long-term investments. The trade-off is the return forgone and the need to size reserves so that too much capital is not left idle.

Option 2

Planned asset sales

Selling investments can provide liquidity without interest expense or lender call risk. A tax-aware sale plan can consider cost basis, holding periods, charitable goals, and portfolio concentration. Sales may realize taxable gains or losses and reduce future market exposure.

Option 3

Securities-backed line of credit

A securities-backed line may let an eligible borrower pledge non-retirement investments as collateral while keeping them invested. Rates, eligible collateral, advance values, and maintenance rules vary by lender. A falling portfolio value can lead to a collateral demand, repayment demand, or sale of pledged assets.

Option 4

Brokerage margin borrowing

Margin borrowing is provided through a brokerage account and is subject to account-specific terms and regulatory requirements. It can create rapid repayment demands: the firm may change maintenance requirements and may liquidate securities to address a shortfall, potentially without allowing the investor to choose what is sold.

Option 5

Home equity or real-estate credit

A home equity line, mortgage, or other real-estate-backed facility may offer another source of capital, subject to property value, underwriting, and repayment capacity. Consider variable rates, fees, refinancing risk, and the consequences of pledging a home or investment property.

Option 6

Business or asset-based credit

Business credit, a commercial line, or lending secured by business assets may be relevant to owners with operating or investment-company needs. Review covenants, guarantees, borrowing-base tests, maturity dates, and whether a business downturn could affect personal finances.

Borrowing against a concentrated stock position

For an investor with a large appreciated holding, borrowing may be considered as one source of temporary liquidity when an outright sale would be mistimed or would realize gains. A loan is not diversification: the investor remains exposed to the stock’s price movements while adding interest cost, lender requirements, and the possibility that falling collateral values trigger a demand to repay or add collateral.

Stress-test what happens if the stock drops sharply, the lender reduces the eligible advance value, rates rise, or the expected repayment source is delayed. Compare that outcome with a staged sale or another source of funds. Avoid assuming that a credit line can be renewed or remain available indefinitely.

Questions to ask before borrowing

  • What is the total cost, including the rate, spread, fees, and any rate-reset terms?
  • Can the lender change collateral values, borrowing limits, or maintenance requirements?
  • What events can trigger a demand for repayment, more collateral, or a forced sale?
  • Are there restrictions on how proceeds may be used, and what covenants or guarantees apply?
  • What is the realistic repayment plan, and what is the backup if that plan changes?
  • How could interest deductibility or other tax treatment depend on the use of proceeds and current law?

Liquidity decisions can affect investment risk, taxes, estate goals, and business continuity at the same time. Frequently asked questions

What is a securities-backed line of credit?

A securities-backed line is a loan facility secured by eligible non-retirement investments. The lender determines eligible assets and collateral values. If collateral value falls or other terms are not met, the borrower may have to add collateral or repay, and pledged assets may be sold.

Does borrowing against investments avoid taxes?

Borrowing is not the same as selling an investment, but tax outcomes depend on the facts and use of proceeds. Interest deductibility and other tax treatment are subject to complex rules and should be discussed with a qualified tax professional.

Is a securities-backed line safer than selling stock?

Neither choice is universally safer. A sale reduces market exposure but may realize taxable gains; borrowing retains market exposure and adds interest and collateral risk. Compare both against the liquidity need, time horizon, tax picture, and a stressed-market scenario.

Can retirement accounts be used as collateral?

Retirement accounts generally have restrictions and should not be assumed to qualify as collateral. Rules vary by account type and lender; confirm the specific treatment with the custodian, lender, and a qualified tax or legal professional.

A practical framework

Compare before committing

  1. Define the amount, timing, and repayment source.
  2. Compare cash, planned sales, and credit—not just the quoted rate.
  3. Stress-test collateral, cash flow, and rate changes.
  4. Review tax, legal, and lender terms with the appropriate professionals.
