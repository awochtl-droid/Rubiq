Depreciation Recapture: The Tax Bill Hiding in Your Rental Property

Depreciation lowers your taxes every year you own a rental. When you sell, the IRS takes some of that benefit back. For long-held property, recapture is often the largest single piece of the tax bill, and the reason many owners look at 1031 exchanges.

Definition

Depreciation recapture is the tax you pay, when you sell a property at a gain, on the depreciation deductions you took while you owned it. For rental and commercial real estate, that portion of the gain is taxed at your ordinary income rate, capped at 25% federally, instead of the lower long-term capital gains rate.

How depreciation recapture works

Each year you own a rental, you can deduct part of the building’s cost: over 27.5 years for residential rental property and 39 years for commercial property. Land is not depreciated. Those deductions reduce your taxable rental income, but they also reduce your adjusted basis in the property.

When you sell, your gain is the sale price minus that lower adjusted basis. The part of the gain that comes from depreciation you took is taxed differently from the rest:

  • Unrecaptured Section 1250 gain: gain equal to straight-line depreciation on buildings. Taxed at your ordinary rate, up to a maximum of 25%.
  • Section 1245 recapture: depreciation on personal property such as appliances, carpeting, and components separated out in a cost segregation study, including bonus depreciation. Taxed as ordinary income, up to 37%.
  • Remaining gain: appreciation above your original cost basis. Taxed at long-term capital gains rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%.

High-income sellers may also owe the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax on the whole gain, and state income tax applies on top. Pennsylvania, for example, taxes the full gain at 3.07%.

A worked example

An investor bought a residential rental for $500,000 ($100,000 land, $400,000 building) and sells it 15 years later for $900,000. They are in the top federal bracket and live in Pennsylvania.

Ways to defer or reduce recapture

Defer

1031 exchange

Exchanging into like-kind real estate, including a Delaware Statutory Trust, carries your basis forward and defers recapture along with the rest of the gain.

Defer and diversify

721 exchange

Contributing property to a REIT’s operating partnership defers the gain until units are converted. See 721 exchanges and UPREITs.

Hold

Step-up at death

Under current law, heirs generally receive a basis equal to fair market value at death, which can eliminate deferred recapture entirely. Pennsylvania inheritance tax may still apply.

Offset

Losses and charitable tools

Harvested capital losses can offset the gain, and some owners use charitable remainder trusts or charitable gifts of property as part of an exit plan.

Two limits are worth knowing. An installment sale can spread capital gain over several years, but Section 1245 recapture is generally taxed in the year of sale regardless of when you are paid. And a Qualified Opportunity Zone investment can defer gain reinvested within the required window, with its own rules and deadlines; our opportunity zone guide covers them.

Special situations

  • You never claimed depreciation. Recapture is based on depreciation “allowed or allowable,” so it can apply even if you skipped the deduction. A CPA can often catch up missed depreciation with an accounting method change before you sell.
  • Selling a former rental that became your home. The home-sale exclusion does not cover depreciation taken after May 6, 1997; that portion is still taxed as recapture.
  • Cost segregation and bonus depreciation. Accelerated deductions boost early cash flow but create more Section 1245 recapture taxed at ordinary rates if you later sell without exchanging.

Frequently asked questions

What is depreciation recapture?

Depreciation recapture is the tax owed on the depreciation deductions you took on a property when you sell it for more than its depreciated basis. For residential and commercial real estate, the gain attributable to straight-line depreciation is called unrecaptured Section 1250 gain and is taxed at your ordinary income rate, capped at 25% federally. Depreciation on personal property and cost-segregated components is Section 1245 recapture, taxed as ordinary income.

What is the depreciation recapture tax rate on rental property?

For real estate depreciated on a straight-line basis, the federal rate on recaptured depreciation is your ordinary income rate up to a maximum of 25%. Higher-income sellers may also owe the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax, plus any state income tax. Gain above the recaptured depreciation is taxed at long-term capital gains rates of 0%, 15%, or 20%.

Can a 1031 exchange avoid depreciation recapture?

A properly completed 1031 exchange defers depreciation recapture along with the rest of the gain by carrying the old property’s basis into the replacement property. The recapture is not eliminated; it can come due when the replacement property is eventually sold in a taxable transaction. Under current law, property held until death may receive a step-up in basis that removes the deferred gain for heirs.

Do I owe depreciation recapture if I never claimed depreciation?

Generally yes. Recapture is based on depreciation allowed or allowable, meaning the depreciation you were entitled to take, even if you did not claim it. Owners who missed depreciation can often correct it with an accounting method change, which a CPA can evaluate.
