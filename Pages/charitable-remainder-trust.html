Charitable Remainder Trusts: Sell Appreciated Assets, Keep the Income, Give the Rest

A charitable remainder trust lets you move appreciated stock, real estate, or a business interest into a trust that sells it without immediate capital gains tax, pays you income for life or a set term, and leaves what remains to charity. Here is how it works and what it costs.

How a charitable remainder trust works

  1. You transfer appreciated assets to an irrevocable trust, well before any sale is negotiated.
  2. The trust sells them. Because it is tax-exempt for federal purposes, the full proceeds stay invested.
  3. You receive payments of 5% to 50% a year, for your life (or joint lives) or a term of up to 20 years.
  4. You take a charitable deduction now for the present value of what charity is expected to receive.
  5. Charity receives what is left when the term ends, which must be projected to be at least 10% of the starting value.

Federal rules under Internal Revenue Code Section 664. Charitable remainder trusts are drafted by an attorney and administered by a trustee.

For assets that produce no income or are hard to sell, such as land or a private business, a net income or “flip” unitrust pays only the trust’s actual income until a trigger event, typically the sale, and then converts to a regular unitrust.

How the income is taxed

The capital gain is not erased. It is deferred and spread over the payments you receive. Each payment is taxed under a four-tier, “worst first” order: ordinary income the trust has earned comes out first, then capital gains, then tax-exempt income, and finally a tax-free return of principal. A trust that sold a low-basis asset will usually pay out capital gain for many years.

Pennsylvania doesn’t treat CRTs as tax-exempt

Pennsylvania does not recognize charitable remainder trusts as tax-exempt. The trust files a Pennsylvania fiduciary return (PA-41) and generally pays Pennsylvania’s 3.07% tax itself on gain it retains, usually in the year of the sale, while Pennsylvania-taxable income it distributes is taxed to you. Some families use a trust sited outside Pennsylvania to change this; confirm the state treatment for your situation with your CPA and attorney.

The 2026 deduction changes

The charitable deduction is limited by your income: generally 30% of adjusted gross income for appreciated property given to a trust whose remainder goes to a public charity, or 20% if a private foundation can be the remainder beneficiary, with five years to carry forward any excess. Starting in 2026, itemizers also lose the first 0.5% of AGI of charitable gifts, and those in the top 37% bracket receive a benefit capped at 35%. The trust’s tax deferral itself is unchanged.

Traps to avoid

  • A sale that is already in motion. If a sale is virtually certain when you transfer the asset, for example after a signed letter of intent or well-advanced negotiations, the IRS and the courts can tax the gain to you even without a binding contract. Fund the trust well before negotiations advance.
  • Debt and business income. Mortgaged real estate and some business or partnership interests can generate unrelated business taxable income, which the trust pays a 100% excise tax on. These assets need careful review.
  • It is irrevocable. You cannot take the assets back, and ending the trust early can be costly.
  • Ongoing administration. The trust files an annual Form 5227, needs a trustee, and incurs legal and accounting costs, so it suits larger gifts.

Alternatives to compare

A donor-advised fund gives a larger, simpler deduction but no income back. A one-time qualified charitable distribution of up to $55,000 in 2026 can fund a separate charitable remainder trust funded only by QCDs, with payments to you or your spouse. For concentrated stock, an exchange fund or prepaid variable forward keeps the value in the family instead of leaving it to charity.

Frequently asked questions

What is a charitable remainder trust?

A charitable remainder trust is an irrevocable trust that pays you, or other beneficiaries, income for life or for a term of up to 20 years, then gives what remains to charity. Because the trust is exempt from federal income tax, it can sell appreciated assets without paying capital gains tax at the time of sale, and you receive an income tax deduction for the present value of the charity’s share.

What is the difference between a CRAT and a CRUT?

A charitable remainder annuity trust (CRAT) pays a fixed dollar amount every year and cannot accept additional contributions. A charitable remainder unitrust (CRUT) pays a fixed percentage of the trust’s value, recalculated each year, so payments rise and fall with the investments, and it can accept additional contributions if the trust allows.

Does a charitable remainder trust avoid capital gains tax?

It defers it. The trust can sell appreciated assets without paying federal capital gains tax at the time of sale, but the gain is carried into the payments you receive, which are taxed under a four-tier ordering rule. Pennsylvania does not treat charitable remainder trusts as tax-exempt, so the trust generally pays Pennsylvania tax on the gain.

How much income can a charitable remainder trust pay?

The annual payout must be between 5% and 50% of the trust’s value, and the projected remainder for charity must be at least 10% of the value contributed. In practice, the payout rate is chosen together with the beneficiaries’ ages and the term so the trust meets those tests.

Can I name my children as beneficiaries of a charitable remainder trust?

Yes, children can receive the income payments, but the remainder must go to charity, and naming younger beneficiaries reduces the charity’s projected share and the income tax deduction. Gifts of income to someone other than you or your spouse can also have gift tax consequences.

Before you fund one

Questions to answer

  1. Is the asset highly appreciated and ready to be sold?
  2. Has any sale already been agreed? Fund the trust first.
  3. Do you want fixed payments or payments that can grow?
  4. Is the asset mortgaged or a business interest?
  5. How will heirs be provided for instead?
  6. Who will serve as trustee and handle the annual filings?
