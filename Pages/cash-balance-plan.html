Cash Balance Plans: How Business Owners Deduct Far More Than a 401(k) Allows

A 401(k) with profit sharing tops out at $72,000 a year in 2026, plus catch-up contributions. A cash balance plan stacked on top can let an owner in their 50s or 60s deduct well over $200,000 more. Here is how the plans work, what they cost, and who they fit.

How a cash balance plan works

A cash balance plan is a defined benefit pension that looks like a savings account. Each participant has a hypothetical account that grows each year by a pay credit (a set percentage of salary or a flat dollar amount) and an interest credit (a fixed rate or one tied to an index). At retirement or separation, the participant can usually take the account as a lump sum and roll it to an IRA.

Behind the accounts, the plan holds one pooled investment portfolio, and the business is responsible for funding it. An enrolled actuary calculates the required contribution each year. Because the limit is on the eventual benefit rather than the annual contribution, older owners, who have fewer years to fund a large benefit, can contribute the most.

A worked example

A 55-year-old physician owns a practice with five staff and pays herself $360,000. She adds a cash balance plan to her existing 401(k) and profit sharing plan.

Employees, testing, and coverage

  • Employees must benefit. The plan must pass nondiscrimination testing, which generally means meaningful contributions for rank-and-file staff. Each year, it must also benefit at least 40% of employees or 50 employees, whichever is fewer.
  • Pairs with a 401(k). When both plans exist, profit sharing contributions are generally limited to 6% of pay, but the employee 401(k) deferral and catch-up are unaffected.
  • PBGC insurance. Many defined benefit plans must pay premiums to the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation. Plans covering only owners, and those of professional service employers that have never had more than 25 active participants, are generally exempt.

Costs and commitment

  • A required contribution. Once adopted, the business must fund the plan each year within the actuary’s range, including making up shortfalls if investments lag the interest credit.
  • Meant to be permanent. There is no fixed minimum number of years, but the IRS expects a plan to be ongoing. Terminating soon after adoption without a business reason can draw scrutiny. Most owners plan on at least several years.
  • Administration. Expect annual fees for a third-party administrator and enrolled actuary, plus a Form 5500 filing.
  • Steady profits help. These plans fit businesses with consistent income and owners who want to save aggressively for 5 to 10 years before retiring or selling.

Deadlines

A new plan can generally be adopted for a year as late as the business’s tax return due date, including extensions, and funded by that date to be deductible. Deciding before year-end still helps: it gives the actuary time to design the plan around your census and lets you plan cash flow for the first contribution.

Pennsylvania differs from federal rules

Pennsylvania doesn’t follow every federal retirement plan deduction. For example, it taxes employees’ 401(k) deferrals. The state savings for owners can differ from the federal figures, so ask your CPA how your contributions are treated for Pennsylvania purposes.

Frequently asked questions

What is a cash balance plan?

A cash balance plan is a type of defined benefit pension plan in which each participant has a hypothetical account that grows with annual pay credits and interest credits. The employer funds the plan and bears the investment risk, and participants can usually take their account as a lump sum when they leave or retire.

How much can a business owner contribute to a cash balance plan in 2026?

It depends mainly on age. The limit applies to the benefit the plan can provide, a maximum annual benefit of $290,000 in 2026, so older owners can contribute more each year. Illustrative maximums run from roughly $120,000 at age 40 to over $300,000 at age 60, before adding a 401(k) and profit sharing.

Can I have a cash balance plan and a 401(k)?

Yes, and most owners do. The 401(k) deferral and catch-up contributions are unaffected, but employer profit sharing contributions are generally limited to 6% of pay when the business sponsors both plans.

Do I have to contribute for my employees?

Generally yes. The plan must pass nondiscrimination testing and cover enough employees, which usually means meaningful contributions for staff. An actuary designs the plan to meet these rules at the lowest reasonable cost.

When is the deadline to set up a cash balance plan?

A new plan can generally be adopted for a tax year as late as the business’s tax return due date, including extensions, and contributions made by that date can be deducted for that year. Starting the design before year-end leaves more room for planning.

Is it a fit?

Good signs

  1. Owner age 45 or older with high, steady income.
  2. Already maxing out a 401(k) and profit sharing.
  3. Small staff, or staff who are younger or lower paid.
  4. Able to commit to contributions for several years.
  5. Planning to retire or sell in 5 to 10 years.
  6. Working with a CPA who can coordinate the deduction.
