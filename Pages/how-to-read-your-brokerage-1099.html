What Your 1099 Says About Your Portfolio: Five Numbers to Check

Your brokerage 1099 arrives in February as a document for your tax preparer. It is also a record of how much tax your portfolio created last year. Here is how to read it, where five telling numbers sit, and what each may say about how your taxable account is invested.

The short answer

Your brokerage 1099 is more than a form for your tax preparer. It is a one-year record of how much taxable income your portfolio produced and what kind. Five numbers on it show whether the way your taxable account is invested may be adding to your tax bill. None of them proves a problem by itself, but each is worth a question.

First, what you are holding

A brokerage firm does not send separate forms for dividends, interest and sales. It combines them into one document, usually called a consolidated or composite 1099. Inside are up to five IRS forms: 1099-DIV (dividends and fund distributions), 1099-INT (interest), 1099-B (sales), 1099-MISC and 1099-OID. The first pages are a summary with the totals your tax return needs. The pages behind them list every transaction.

Only taxable accounts get one. IRAs and other retirement accounts do not produce a consolidated 1099, because income inside them is not taxed each year. If you took money out of an IRA you will get a separate Form 1099-R.

1. Capital gain distributions (1099-DIV, box 2a)

When a mutual fund sells holdings at a profit, it must pass the gain to its shareholders, usually in December. You owe tax on that distribution even if you reinvested it, even if you bought the fund a month earlier, and even if the fund’s price fell during the year.

  • What it may mean: actively managed mutual funds held in a taxable account. Index funds and most exchange-traded funds distribute far less.
  • What to ask: which funds produced the number, and would a lower-turnover fund or an ETF fill the same role? Selling a fund to replace it can trigger its own gain, so the answer depends on your cost basis.

2. Ordinary dividends far above qualified (box 1a compared with 1b)

Box 1a is every dividend you received. Box 1b is the part that qualifies for the lower long-term capital gains rates. The difference is taxed at your ordinary income rate, which for high earners is roughly double.

  • What it may mean: bond funds, real estate investment trusts, money market funds or high-turnover stock funds in the taxable account. Their payouts are mostly not qualified.
  • What to ask: could those holdings sit in an IRA or 401(k) instead, with the more tax-efficient holdings in the taxable account? That is asset location, and it changes where you hold things, not what you own.

3. Short-term gains (1099-B, short-term section)

Form 1099-B sorts every sale by how long you held the investment. A gain on something held one year or less is short-term and taxed at ordinary rates. Held one day longer, the same gain would have been long-term.

  • What it may mean: frequent trading, a manager who rebalances without regard to holding periods, or a strategy with high turnover by design.
  • What to ask: were the short-term sales necessary, and were losses elsewhere in the account available to offset them?

4. Wash sale adjustments (1099-B, box 1g)

If you sell at a loss and buy the same or a substantially identical investment within 30 days before or after, the IRS disallows the loss for now. It is added to the cost basis of the replacement shares, so it is usually deferred, not lost. A number in box 1g means that happened.

  • What it may mean: automatic dividend reinvestment buying back shares you just sold, or tax-loss harvesting done without checking the calendar.
  • What the form will not show: your broker only tracks wash sales inside one account. A sale in one account and a repurchase in another, including your IRA or your spouse’s account, is still a wash sale, and it is yours to report.

5. Taxable interest (1099-INT, box 1)

Interest from cash sweeps, CDs, corporate bonds and most bond funds is taxed at your ordinary rate. Two other boxes matter here: box 3 is interest on U.S. Treasury obligations, which most states do not tax, and box 8 is tax-exempt interest, mostly from municipal bonds.

  • What it may mean: a large cash balance or taxable bond position held by someone in a high bracket, with nothing in boxes 3 or 8.
  • What to ask: after tax, would Treasuries or municipal bonds leave you with more? Our tax-equivalent yield calculator does that comparison.

A worked example

A couple in the top federal bracket holds $1.5 million in a taxable account. Their 1099 shows $40,000 of capital gain distributions and $30,000 of interest and non-qualified dividends.

Amount
Capital gain distributions: $40,000 at 20% plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax$9,520
Interest and non-qualified dividends: $30,000 at 37% plus 3.8%$12,240
Federal tax on income they did not choose to take$21,760

That is about 1.45% of the account in one year, before state tax, and before any gains from sales they chose to make. Not all of it could be avoided, and changing holdings has its own tax cost. But a cost of that size is worth measuring the same way you would measure a fund’s expense ratio.

Before you file: three checks

  • Wait for corrections. Funds and REITs often reclassify income after year-end, and firms reissue the 1099. If you hold either, filing in the first days of February can mean amending later.
  • Check the cost basis. For older positions and transferred accounts, the basis may be blank or marked as not reported to the IRS (box 12 unchecked, or box 5 checked). You are responsible for supplying the right number.
  • Look for foreign tax paid. An amount in 1099-DIV box 7 may be claimed as a credit. It is easy to miss on a summary page.

What to do with what you find

The useful comparison is not this year’s form alone but the pattern: the same funds distributing gains every December, or interest income rising as cash builds up. The cost of that pattern is called tax drag, and our tax drag quick check gives a rough estimate in about 90 seconds.

Frequently asked questions

What is a consolidated or composite 1099?

It is a single document from a brokerage firm that combines several IRS forms for one taxable account: Form 1099-DIV for dividends and fund distributions, 1099-INT for interest, 1099-B for sales, and sometimes 1099-MISC and 1099-OID. Firms use different names for it, but the IRS boxes are the same.

When do brokerage 1099s arrive?

Most consolidated 1099s are issued in mid-February, later than the January forms from banks and employers. Accounts holding funds or real estate investment trusts that reclassify income after year-end may receive the form later, or receive a corrected one.

What is the difference between ordinary and qualified dividends on Form 1099-DIV?

Box 1a reports total ordinary dividends. Box 1b reports the portion of that total that is qualified, meaning it is taxed at the lower long-term capital gains rates. The difference between the two boxes is taxed at ordinary income rates.

Why do I owe tax on a mutual fund I did not sell?

Mutual funds must distribute the net gains they realize when they sell holdings. Those capital gain distributions, reported in Form 1099-DIV box 2a, are taxable to shareholders in a taxable account in the year paid, even when they are reinvested.

What does a wash sale adjustment on Form 1099-B mean?

An amount in box 1g means a loss was disallowed because the same or a substantially identical security was bought within 30 days before or after the sale. The disallowed loss is generally added to the cost basis of the replacement shares. Brokers track wash sales only within a single account.

Why did I receive a corrected 1099?

Issuers such as funds and real estate investment trusts sometimes change how they classify a distribution after the original form is sent, and cost basis information can be updated. The firm then issues a corrected form. If you already filed, ask your tax preparer whether an amended return is needed.

Have these in hand

1099 review checklist

  • The 1099 for every taxable account, including any corrected version.
  • The summary page totals: 1099-DIV boxes 1a, 1b and 2a; 1099-INT boxes 1, 3 and 8.
  • The 1099-B short-term and long-term totals, and box 1g.
  • A list of what each account holds.
  • Last year’s tax return, to see your bracket.
  • Any loss carryforward from prior years.
