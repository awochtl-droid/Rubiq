GRATs and Concentrated Stock: A Planning Guide

A grantor retained annuity trust may be considered when a family is planning a potential transfer of future appreciation. It is not a stock-diversification tool or a tax-free exit. Understand the mechanics, risks, and professional roles before evaluating whether it belongs in a broader plan.

A GRAT can be one component of multigenerational estate planning for appreciated shares, but the grantor keeps market exposure during the trust term and must receive the required annuity. Outcomes depend on the trust design, stock performance, valuation rules, and the grantor's circumstances.

At a glance

A GRAT transfers property to an irrevocable trust while retaining an annuity for a stated term. Any value remaining for beneficiaries depends on how the assets perform relative to the annuity and applicable Section 7520 valuation assumptions. The strategy can fail to transfer meaningful value, does not itself diversify the stock, and requires legal and tax advice.

What is a grantor retained annuity trust?

A grantor retained annuity trust (GRAT) is an irrevocable trust funded with property such as publicly traded shares. The grantor retains the right to receive specified annuity payments for the trust term. If trust assets remain after the required payments, the remainder passes to the named beneficiaries under the trust terms.

For federal gift-tax valuation, Internal Revenue Code Section 2702 and related rules apply to the interests transferred and retained. A Section 7520 rate is one prescribed assumption used in valuing certain annuity and remainder interests. A tax attorney or other qualified estate-planning attorney must determine how those rules apply, value the transfer, prepare the trust, and advise on reporting. The rate is not a forecast or guaranteed investment return.

Why a family might consider one for concentrated shares

When a family already has a multigenerational transfer objective, a GRAT may be considered for appreciated stock if the family's planning team believes the potential remainder, costs, and risks warrant further analysis. In simplified terms, growth that exceeds the assumptions used to value the retained annuity may leave value for beneficiaries after payments. That possibility is uncertain and depends on actual results and the trust's terms.

A GRAT should not be treated as a shortcut for reducing single-company risk. Shares placed in the trust remain exposed to the issuer's performance unless the trustee can and does diversify them consistent with the trust and applicable law. An estate-transfer plan may be only one part of addressing a concentrated position; review broader approaches in our guide to managing concentrated stock risk.

Assets grow beyond assumptions

After the annuity payments and expenses, a remainder may pass to beneficiaries. The amount is uncertain and depends on actual performance and trust terms.

Assets grow less or decline

Annuity payments can consume much or all of the trust property; there may be little or no remainder for beneficiaries. Concentration risk remains.

Grantor dies during the term

Death during the retained-interest period can affect estate-tax treatment. Counsel should model this risk using the grantor's health, goals, and proposed term.

What a GRAT does not do

  • It does not diversify by itself. The trust may continue to hold a single stock and its full market risk.
  • It does not guarantee a gift-tax result. Transfer value, annuity design, interest-rate assumptions, reporting, and applicable law matter.
  • It does not make a later stock sale tax-free. A sale of appreciated shares can trigger tax; basis and grantor-trust income-tax treatment need individual review.
  • It does not assure a remainder for heirs. Market performance, annuity payments, fees, and the grantor's survival can all change the outcome.

Keep the planning objective clear. A GRAT addresses a potential transfer of future appreciation. It should be compared with other estate-planning and portfolio choices—not presented as a guaranteed tax-saving or stock-risk solution.

Common questions about GRATs and concentrated stock

Does a GRAT diversify concentrated stock?

No. A GRAT is an estate-transfer technique, not a diversification strategy. The trust continues to hold the contributed stock unless assets are sold or otherwise changed, and the grantor remains exposed to the stock's market risk during the term.

Does a GRAT eliminate capital gains tax?

A GRAT does not by itself eliminate capital-gains tax or make a later sale tax-free. A sale of appreciated stock may have tax consequences, and grantor-trust income-tax treatment, basis, and the transaction's facts should be reviewed with a tax professional.

What if the stock does not outperform the Section 7520 rate?

If trust assets do not grow sufficiently after annuity payments and expenses, little or no value may remain for beneficiaries at the end of the term. The result depends on actual investment performance, the trust terms, applicable valuation assumptions, and other facts; it is not guaranteed.
