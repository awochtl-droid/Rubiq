Are Buffer ETFs Worth the Hype?

Buffered ETFs limit how much you can lose in a year, and in exchange limit how much you can gain. We tested how five common designs would have done in every 12-month period since 1871, after fees, and compared them with simply owning the S&P 500 or holding Treasury bills. The full data is at the bottom of the page so you can check our work.

The short answer

  • Returns landed between cash and stocks. Over the last 50 years the buffered designs earned 3.5% to 6.2% a year after fees, against 4.3% for T-bills and 11.8% for the S&P 500.
  • They lost money less often, but not never. A 15% buffer still lost 31.3% in the 12 months from March 2008.
  • Full protection behaved like cash. With a 6% cap, it beat T-bills in only 44% of 12-month periods after fees.
  • The cap drives the result. These figures assume the same cap every year. Real caps reset every year and have nearly doubled from one year to the next.

Why buffered ETFs exist

Buffered products were built for people who are close to retirement or already in it. A large loss right before or early in retirement is hard to recover from, because withdrawals continue while the portfolio is down. Many of these investors want to stay in stocks for growth but can’t afford a 2008-style drawdown.

  • 2010: buffered annuities. Insurers first offered this design inside annuities. Equitable launched the first buffered annuity, now called a registered index-linked annuity, in October 2010, when low interest rates had made traditional guaranteed products less generous (Society of Actuaries).
  • 2018: buffered ETFs. Innovator Capital Management launched the first buffered ETFs, bringing the same idea to a fund that trades daily, has no surrender charges, and costs less than most annuities (etf.com).
  • 2022 onward: rapid growth. Buffered ETFs held about $17 billion by September 2022, with inflows rising as stocks and bonds fell together that year (etf.com). In its optimistic scenario, Cerulli Associates projects the category could quadruple by 2030, driven largely by baby boomers moving into retirement and near-retirees looking for predictability (Cerulli, November 2025).

The question for that investor is whether the protection is worth what it costs. That’s what the rest of this page tests.

How a buffered ETF works

A buffered ETF, also called a defined-outcome ETF, uses options on an index, usually the S&P 500, to reshape its return over a fixed outcome period, typically 12 months. At the start of each period the fund sets two terms:

  • The cap is the most you can earn for the period. If the index rises 25% and the cap is 10%, you get 10%.
  • The buffer is how much of a decline the fund absorbs. With a 15% buffer, an index drop of 12% costs you nothing; a drop of 20% costs you 5%.

Some funds add a dual-direction feature: an index loss that stays inside the buffer is paid out as a gain of the same size, so a 7% decline pays +7%. Others offer full protection, where any decline returns 0%, in exchange for a much lower cap. When the period ends, the fund resets with new terms based on interest rates and option prices at that time.

Two costs are easy to miss. The payoff is based on the index’s price return, so you give up its dividends. And the fund charges an expense ratio, which we assume is 0.85% a year.

The biggest assumption: a fixed cap

Every result on this page assumes the same cap in every year: 10% for the 10/10 and 15/10 designs, 8% for the 15/8, and 6% for full protection. Real funds don’t work that way. At each annual reset the fund buys and sells a new set of options, and the cap is whatever those options can pay for at that moment. The main factors:

  • Interest rates. Higher rates generally allow higher caps.
  • Market volatility. Higher expected volatility has generally allowed higher caps on buffer funds, since the options the fund sells are worth more (Advisor Perspectives).
  • Buffer size. A deeper buffer costs more, so it comes with a lower cap.
  • The index’s dividend yield and the fund’s fee also reduce what’s left for the cap.

The swings can be large. For the same 15%-buffer S&P 500 fund, Innovator estimated a cap of 8.54% to 10.34% for 2022 (December 2021). After a year of rising rates and high volatility, the 2023 cap was set at 18.84% (January 2023). Both figures are before the fund’s 0.79% fee.

Practical points before buying

  • Compare with what cash pays today. A fully protected fund only makes sense when its cap, less the fee, clearly beats the Treasury bill or CD yield for the same period.
  • Buy at or near the reset date. Buying mid-period gives you the remaining cap and buffer, which can be very different from the headline terms. Issuers publish them daily.
  • Don’t pick a month; spread them. No start month was reliably better. Splitting money across several reset months smooths the effect of any single crash.
  • Mind the taxes. In a taxable account, ETF gains held over a year are generally taxed as long-term capital gains, while T-bill interest is ordinary income. Inside an IRA, switching funds has no tax cost.
  • Match the horizon. Annual buffers fit money you may need in one to five years. For longer horizons, the data favored owning the index or a longer-term buffered design.

Methodology and data

Hypothetical results

These are back-tested, hypothetical results of rules applied to historical index data, not the performance of any actual fund or account. Buffered ETFs are a recent product, and none existed for nearly all of this period. Actual results would have differed because of changing caps, trading costs, and taxes. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

How each period is calculated

  1. Each 12-month outcome period starts at a month-end index level and ends 12 months later. A new period starts every month, from February 1871 to October 2025: 1,857 periods in all.
  2. The S&P 500 price return for the period equals its total return minus the trailing dividend yield at the start.
  3. Each structure’s payoff is applied to that price return, then the 0.85% expense ratio is subtracted.
  4. Treasury bill returns compound the monthly 3-month T-bill yield (yield ÷ 12 each month) over the same 12 months.
  5. Six-year results compound six consecutive 12-month periods that share the same start month.
R = S&P 500 price return over the period

Dual-direction (buffer B, cap C):
  if R ≥ 0          payoff = min(R, C)
  if −B ≤ R < 0     payoff = −R
  if R < −B         payoff = R + B

Full protection (cap C):
  any R             payoff = min(max(R, 0), C)

Net return = payoff − 0.85%

Six-year term (P = six-year price return):
  if P ≥ 0          payoff = 1.07 × P
  if −20% ≤ P < 0   payoff = 0
  if P < −20%       payoff = P + 20%

Limitations

  • Caps and buffers are held constant. Real caps reset each year with interest rates, volatility and option prices, and have nearly doubled from one year to the next. See the cap section for how results change with the cap.
  • Before July 2003 the index uses monthly average prices rather than month-end closes, which slightly smooths 12-month returns and understates how often caps and buffers were breached.
  • After June 2023 the dividend yield is held at its last reported level.
  • “Last 20 years” and “last 50 years” use the most recent 20 or 50 complete periods for each start month. “Since 1934” uses every period starting in January 1934 or later.
  • No taxes, advisory fees, or trading costs are included.

Frequently asked questions

Are buffered ETFs worth it?

It depends on the job. Over the last 50 years, the buffered structures we tested earned about 3.5% to 6.2% a year after fees, compared with 4.3% for Treasury bills and 11.8% for the S&P 500. They lost money less often than stocks, but they gave up most of the gain in strong years and still lost heavily in deep bear markets such as 2008. They fit money with a one-to-five-year horizon better than long-term growth money.

What is the difference between a buffer and full downside protection?

A buffer absorbs only the first part of a loss, such as 10% or 15%. If the index falls further, you lose the rest one for one. Full (100%) protection means any decline returns 0% for the period, but the cap on gains is much lower, around 6% in our test.

What does dual-direction mean?

In a dual-direction structure, an index decline that stays inside the buffer is paid out as a gain of the same size. For example, with a 10% buffer, an S&P 500 return of minus 7% pays plus 7%. A decline larger than the buffer becomes a loss.

Does the start month of a buffered ETF matter?

Not in any predictable way. In our data, each start month compounded within about half a percentage point of the others over 50 years, and the months that led in one period did not lead in the next. The cap available at the reset date matters more, and spreading money across several reset months reduces the impact of any single bad year.

Do buffered ETFs pay dividends?

Generally no. Their payoffs are based on the S&P 500 price return, so the dividends an index fund collects, roughly 1.5% to 2% a year recently, are given up. That cost appears in every year that finishes below the cap.

How do buffered ETFs compare with Treasury bills or CDs?

Over the last 50 years the 15% buffer with a 10% cap beat Treasury bills in 73% of 12-month periods. The fully protected structure with a 6% cap beat them in only 44%, because its fee eats into a low cap. Compare a protected ETF with the T-bill or CD yield available on the day you buy.
