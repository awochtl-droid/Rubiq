Are Buffer ETFs Worth the Hype?

Buffered ETFs didn’t exist before 2018. By the end of 2025 they held $78 billion across 420 funds, driven largely by investors nearing or in retirement. We tested three common designs in every 12-month period since 1871, after fees, against simply owning the S&P 500 or holding Treasury bills. The full data is at the bottom of the page so you can check our work.

  • 2010: the idea starts in annuities. Equitable launched the first buffered annuity, now called a registered index-linked annuity, in October 2010, when low interest rates had made traditional guaranteed products less generous (Society of Actuaries).
  • 2018: the first buffered ETFs. Innovator Capital Management brought the same idea to a fund that trades daily, has no surrender charges, and costs less than most annuities (etf.com).
  • 2022: the breakout year. When stocks and bonds fell together, money poured in. The category held about $17 billion across roughly 150 funds by September 2022 (etf.com).
  • 2025 and beyond. Assets more than quadrupled from 2022 to the end of 2025. In its optimistic scenario, Cerulli Associates projects the category could quadruple again by 2030 (Cerulli, November 2025).

Who’s buying

According to Cerulli, much of the demand comes from advisors serving clients near or in retirement, as baby boomers retire and near-retirees look for predictability. A large loss right before or early in retirement is hard to recover from, because withdrawals continue while the portfolio is down. Many of these investors want to stay in stocks for growth but can’t afford a 2008-style drawdown.

So is the protection worth what it costs? That’s what the rest of this page tests.

Are they worth it? The short answer

  • Returns landed between cash and stocks. Over the last 50 years the three designs earned 3.5% to 7.5% a year after fees, against 4.3% for T-bills and 11.8% for the S&P 500.
  • The standard 15% buffer earned the most (7.5%) because its 15% cap kept more of the strong years.
  • The dual-direction buffer was the most consistent, positive in 90% of 12-month periods. Both 15% buffers still lost 31.3% from March 2008.
  • Full protection never lost more than its fee, but it compounded below T-bills.
  • The cap drives everything. We assume a fixed cap; real caps reset every year and have nearly doubled from one year to the next.

How a buffered ETF works

A buffered ETF, also called a defined-outcome ETF, uses options on an index, usually the S&P 500, to reshape its return over a fixed outcome period, typically 12 months. At the start of each period the fund sets two terms:

  • The cap is the most you can earn for the period. If the index rises 25% and the cap is 10%, you get 10%.
  • The buffer is how much of a decline the fund absorbs. With a 15% buffer, an index drop of 12% costs you nothing; a drop of 20% costs you 5%.

Some funds add a dual-direction feature: an index loss that stays inside the buffer is paid out as a gain of the same size, so a 7% decline pays +7%. Others offer full protection, where any decline returns 0%, in exchange for a much lower cap. When the period ends, the fund resets with new terms based on interest rates and option prices at that time.

Two costs are easy to miss. The payoff is based on the index’s price return, so you give up its dividends. And the fund charges an expense ratio, which we assume is 0.85% a year.

The biggest assumption: a fixed cap

Every result on this page assumes the same cap in every year: 6% for full protection, 15% for the standard buffer, and 10% for the dual-direction buffer. Real funds don’t work that way. At each annual reset the fund buys and sells a new set of options, and the cap is whatever those options can pay for at that moment. The main factors:

  • Interest rates. Higher rates generally allow higher caps.
  • Market volatility. Higher expected volatility has generally allowed higher caps on buffer funds, since the options the fund sells are worth more (Advisor Perspectives).
  • Buffer design. A deeper buffer, full protection, or a dual-direction feature each cost more, so they come with lower caps.
  • The index’s dividend yield and the fund’s fee also reduce what’s left for the cap.

The swings can be large. For a standard 15%-buffer S&P 500 fund, design 2 above, Innovator estimated a cap of 8.54% to 10.34% for 2022 (December 2021). After a year of rising rates and high volatility, the 2023 cap was set at 18.84% (January 2023). Both figures are before the fund’s 0.79% fee. Our 15% assumption sits between those two years.

Practical points before buying

  • Compare with what cash pays today. A fully protected fund only makes sense when its cap, less the fee, clearly beats the Treasury bill or CD yield for the same period.
  • Buy at or near the reset date. Buying mid-period gives you the remaining cap and buffer, which can be very different from the headline terms. Issuers publish them daily.
  • Don’t pick a month; spread them. No start month was reliably better. Splitting money across several reset months smooths the effect of any single crash.
  • Mind the taxes. In a taxable account, ETF gains held over a year are generally taxed as long-term capital gains, while T-bill interest is ordinary income. Inside an IRA, switching funds has no tax cost.
  • Match the horizon. Annual buffers fit money you may need in one to five years. For longer horizons, the data favored owning the index.

Methodology and data

Hypothetical results

These are back-tested, hypothetical results of rules applied to historical index data, not the performance of any actual fund or account. Buffered ETFs are a recent product, and none existed for nearly all of this period. Actual results would have differed because of changing caps, trading costs, and taxes. Past performance does not guarantee future results.

How each period is calculated

  1. Each 12-month outcome period starts at a month-end index level and ends 12 months later. A new period starts every month, from February 1871 to October 2025: 1,857 periods in all.
  2. The S&P 500 price return for the period equals its total return minus the trailing dividend yield at the start.
  3. Each design’s payoff is applied to that price return, then the 0.85% expense ratio is subtracted.
  4. Treasury bill returns compound the monthly 3-month T-bill yield (yield ÷ 12 each month) over the same 12 months.
  5. Six-year results compound six consecutive 12-month periods that share the same start month.
R = S&P 500 price return over the period

Full protection (cap C):
  any R             payoff = min(max(R, 0), C)

Standard buffer (buffer B, cap C):
  if R ≥ 0          payoff = min(R, C)
  if −B ≤ R < 0     payoff = 0
  if R < −B         payoff = R + B

Dual-direction buffer (buffer B, cap C):
  if R ≥ 0          payoff = min(R, C)
  if −B ≤ R < 0     payoff = −R
  if R < −B         payoff = R + B

Net return = payoff − 0.85%

Limitations

  • Caps and buffers are held constant. Real caps reset each year with interest rates, volatility and option prices, and have nearly doubled from one year to the next. See the cap section for how results change with the cap.
  • Before July 2003 the index uses monthly average prices rather than month-end closes, which slightly smooths 12-month returns and understates how often caps and buffers were breached.
  • After June 2023 the dividend yield is held at its last reported level.
  • “Last 20 years” and “last 50 years” use the most recent 20 or 50 complete periods for each start month. “Since 1934” uses every period starting in January 1934 or later.
  • No taxes, advisory fees, or trading costs are included.

Frequently asked questions

Are buffer ETFs worth it?

It depends on the job. Over the last 50 years, the three designs we tested earned 3.5% to 7.5% a year after fees, compared with 4.3% for Treasury bills and 11.8% for the S&P 500. The buffers lost money less often than stocks, but gave up much of the gain in strong years, and the 15% buffers still lost about 31% in the 12 months from March 2008. They fit money with a one-to-five-year horizon better than long-term growth money.

What is the difference between a buffer and full protection?

A buffer absorbs only the first part of a loss, 15% in our examples. If the index falls further, you lose the rest one for one. Full protection means any decline returns 0% for the period, but the cap on gains is much lower, 6% in our test.

What does dual-direction mean?

In a dual-direction buffer, an index decline that stays inside the buffer is paid out as a gain of the same size. With a 15% buffer, an S&P 500 return of minus 7% pays plus 7%. A decline larger than the buffer becomes a loss. The feature costs something, so dual-direction funds usually have lower caps than standard buffers with the same cushion.

Which is better, a standard buffer or a dual-direction buffer?

With the caps we assumed, the standard 15% buffer with a 15% cap compounded faster over the last 50 years (7.5% against 6.2%), because it kept more in strong years. The dual-direction buffer was more consistent: positive in 90% of 12-month periods against 76%. Both lost the same amount in deep declines. The answer depends on the caps on offer when you buy.

Do buffer ETFs pay dividends?

Generally no. Their payoffs are based on the S&P 500 price return, so the dividends an index fund collects, roughly 1.5% to 2% a year recently, are given up.

How do buffer ETFs compare with Treasury bills or CDs?

Over the last 50 years the dual-direction buffer beat Treasury bills in 73% of 12-month periods and the standard buffer in 70%. Full protection with a 6% cap beat them in only 44%, because its fee eats into a low cap. Compare a protected ETF with the T-bill or CD yield available on the day you buy.
