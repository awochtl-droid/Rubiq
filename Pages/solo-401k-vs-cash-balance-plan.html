Solo 401(k) vs. Cash Balance Plan: Which One, or Both?

A solo 401(k) lets an owner with no employees put away up to $75,000 in 2027. A cash balance plan can allow several times that. Here is how the two compare, what changes when you use both, and the deadlines that matter before year-end.

The short answer

A solo 401(k) is the right starting point for almost every owner with no employees: it is cheap, flexible, and allows up to $75,000 in 2027 before catch-up contributions. A cash balance plan makes sense once you are already filling the solo 401(k), are roughly 45 or older, and can commit to large contributions for several years. Most owners who use a cash balance plan keep the solo 401(k) and run both.

How a solo 401(k) works

A solo 401(k) is a regular 401(k) for a business with no employees other than the owner and a spouse. You contribute in two roles:

  • As the employee: up to $25,500 in 2027, plus a catch-up of $8,500 at age 50 or $11,750 at ages 60 to 63. This can be pre-tax or Roth.
  • As the employer: up to 25% of your W-2 pay if the business is an S or C corporation, or about 20% of net self-employment earnings if you are a sole proprietor or partner.

The two together cannot exceed $75,000 in 2027, not counting catch-up contributions. A spouse who works in the business can contribute on the same terms, which can double the household total.

How a cash balance plan is different

A cash balance plan is a pension. The IRS limits the benefit it can pay at retirement, not the yearly deposit, so the allowed contribution rises with age. An actuary calculates the range each year, and the business is expected to fund within it. The assets are invested conservatively because the business must make up any shortfall against the plan’s stated interest credit. Our cash balance plan guide covers the mechanics.

What happens when you run both

This is the detail most comparisons skip. In a plan that covers only owners, the cash balance plan is not insured by the PBGC. When that is the case, the deductible employer contribution to the solo 401(k) is generally limited to 6% of pay in any year you also fund the cash balance plan. Your employee deferral and catch-up are not affected.

Which one fits

  • Solo 401(k) alone: you are under about 45, your income varies, you want Roth contributions, or $75,000 a year already covers what you want to save.
  • Add a cash balance plan: you are filling the solo 401(k), your income has been high and steady, you are in a top bracket, and you can fund the plan for at least three to five years.
  • Neither, yet: you plan to hire employees soon. A solo 401(k) has to be converted to a standard plan once you have eligible staff, and a cash balance plan has to cover them.

Deadlines for this tax year

  • S and C corporation owners: employee deferrals come out of W-2 pay, so they have to be run through payroll by December 31. Employer contributions can be made up to the business’s tax-filing deadline, including extensions.
  • Sole proprietors: the plan can generally be adopted, and first-year contributions made, up to your tax-filing deadline. Having the plan and your deferral election in place by December 31 avoids questions.
  • Cash balance plans: can be adopted as late as the business’s tax-filing deadline, including extensions, but the actuary needs time. Starting before year-end is easier.

Things owners miss

  • Form 5500-EZ. Once solo 401(k) assets exceed $250,000 at year-end, you must file this form each year. Penalties for missing it are steep.
  • Roth catch-up rule. If your W-2 wages from the business were above the IRS threshold in the prior year ($150,000 for 2026 contributions), catch-up contributions must be Roth.
  • One deferral limit. The employee deferral limit is per person across all 401(k) plans, including one at a day job.
  • Mega backdoor Roth. Some solo 401(k) documents allow after-tax contributions and in-plan conversions. See our mega backdoor Roth guide.

Frequently asked questions

What is the solo 401(k) contribution limit for 2027?

For 2027 the employee deferral limit is $25,500, with a catch-up of $8,500 at age 50 or $11,750 at ages 60 to 63. Employee and employer contributions together are capped at $75,000 before catch-ups, or $83,500 at 50 and over and $86,750 at ages 60 to 63.

Can I have a solo 401(k) and a cash balance plan at the same time?

Yes, and most owners who use a cash balance plan keep their solo 401(k). In an owner-only plan that is not covered by the PBGC, the deductible employer contribution to the 401(k) is generally limited to 6% of pay in years the cash balance plan is funded. Employee deferrals are not affected.

Which is better, a solo 401(k) or a cash balance plan?

A solo 401(k) is the better first plan for most owners because it is inexpensive and contributions are optional. A cash balance plan is worth adding when you already fill the solo 401(k), are about 45 or older, have steady high income, and can commit to funding it for several years.

How much can I contribute to a cash balance plan?

It depends mainly on your age and pay, and an actuary sets the figure. As a rough guide from Milliman’s 2026 table, maximums run from about $159,000 at age 45 to $262,000 at 55 and $349,000 at 65. The benefit a plan can fund rises to $300,000 for 2027.

What is the deadline to open a solo 401(k)?

A new plan can generally be adopted up to the business’s tax-filing deadline for the year. S and C corporation owners must make employee deferrals through payroll by December 31. Sole proprietors with a new plan can generally make first-year deferrals up to their filing deadline. Confirm your dates with your CPA.

Do I have to file anything for a solo 401(k)?

Yes, once plan assets exceed $250,000 at the end of the year you must file Form 5500-EZ annually, generally by July 31 for calendar-year plans. A final return is also due when the plan is closed.

Before year-end

Solo owner checklist

  • Confirm you have no eligible employees.
  • Estimate this year’s W-2 pay or net self-employment income.
  • Run employee deferrals through payroll by December 31.
  • Ask an actuary for a cash balance illustration.
  • Check whether your catch-up must be Roth.
  • File Form 5500-EZ if assets are over $250,000.
