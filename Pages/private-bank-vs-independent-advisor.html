Choosing an Advisor

Private Bank vs. Independent Advisor: What Each Offers UHNW Families

Private banks bundle lending, investments, and trust services under one roof. Independent advisors separate advice from the products and the balance sheet. Here is how the two compare on alternative investments, credit, concentrated stock, cost, and conflicts, and why many families end up using both.

The choice is less about which institution is bigger and more about how advice is structured: who selects the investments, who provides the credit, how each party is paid, and who is responsible for making the tax, estate, and investment pieces work together.

These are general tendencies, not rules. Services, fees, and conflicts vary widely by firm, so compare specific proposals rather than labels.

Alternative investments: what private banks offer and what you can access elsewhere

A common reason families stay with a private bank is access to private equity, private credit, real estate, and hedge fund strategies at minimums below what the managers accept directly. Banks typically deliver this through feeder funds that pool client money into a single investment, plus structured notes and, for the largest relationships, occasional co-investments.

That access is real, but it is worth asking how it works. Feeder funds can add a layer of fees on top of the manager's own. The menu is selected by the bank, and the bank or its partners may earn more from some offerings than others. Many independent advisors now reach similar categories of private funds through third-party platforms and institutional share classes, which allows comparing managers across the market rather than within one firm's shelf.

For any alternative investment, wherever it comes from, ask about total fees, liquidity terms, how valuations are produced, and how the position fits the rest of your portfolio. See our guides to private credit and alternatives for UHNW investors and how we approach alternative investments.

Credit and lending for UHNW families

Lending is where private banks are strongest. They can offer securities-based lines, mortgages on high-value or unusual properties, and custom credit facilities from their own balance sheet, sometimes with pricing that improves as more assets move to the bank.

That pricing link is the trade-off to examine. A lower loan rate tied to moving investments can be worth it, or it can be offset by higher investment fees or a narrower investment menu. An independent advisor does not lend, but can help compare a bank's credit terms with other sources, including the custodian's securities-backed lending, and stress-test what happens if collateral values fall. For a fuller comparison of options, read UHNW lending and liquidity strategies.

Holding a large single stock position

Executives, founders, and long-term holders often turn to a private bank because it can do several things in-house: lend against the shares, hedge them with collars or prepaid variable forwards through its trading desk, offer access to exchange funds, and set up charitable or estate vehicles.

An independent advisor works with the same set of strategies but separates the advice from the execution. That can make it easier to compare hedge pricing from more than one counterparty, weigh exchange fund sponsors against each other, or conclude that a staged sale is simpler than any structured solution. Whichever route you take, each strategy carries different tax, liquidity, and counterparty trade-offs. Cost and conflicts of interest

Headline fees rarely tell the full story. To compare fairly, ask each firm for the all-in annual cost on your actual portfolio: the advisory or wealth management fee, underlying fund expenses, platform or placement fees on alternatives, structured product embedded costs, trading and custody fees, and loan spreads.

Then ask how the firm and the individual adviser are paid. Revenue from proprietary funds, lending, product placement, or revenue-sharing arrangements can create incentives that are not visible in the fee schedule. Registered investment advisers must disclose material conflicts in their Form ADV, and broker-dealers provide a Form CRS summary; both are worth reading before you decide.

Many families use both

The choice does not have to be all or nothing. A common arrangement keeps the private bank for mortgages, credit lines, or trust administration, while an independent advisor manages the investment portfolio and coordinates tax, estate, and risk planning across every account. The bank provides the balance sheet; the advisor provides advice that is not tied to it.

At Rubiq Financial Partners, client assets are held at an independent third-party custodian, primarily Charles Schwab, and we work alongside existing banking relationships rather than requiring everything to move. For a real example of how this works, read our private wealth client case study.

Questions to ask either type of firm

  • What is my total annual cost, in dollars, across every layer of fees and spreads?
  • Are you acting as a fiduciary on every account and recommendation, or only some?
  • Do you or your affiliates earn more from any investment, product, or loan you might recommend?
  • How is the alternative investment menu selected, and can I invest outside it?
  • Does my loan pricing depend on keeping assets with you? What happens if I move them?
  • Who coordinates with my CPA and estate attorney, and how often?
  • If I leave, can my positions, including private funds, be transferred in kind?

Frequently asked questions

What alternative investments can private banks offer that I cannot access on my own?

Private banks often offer feeder funds into private equity, private credit, real estate, and hedge fund managers at lower minimums than investing directly, along with structured notes and occasional co-investment opportunities. Many independent advisors can access similar categories of private funds through third-party platforms and institutional share classes. The useful question is not only what is on the menu, but how the menu is chosen, what layered fees apply, and whether the firm earns more from some options than others.

Can I keep my private bank for lending and use an independent advisor for investments?

Yes. Many ultra-high-net-worth families keep a private bank relationship for mortgages, credit lines, or trust services while an independent advisor manages investments and coordinates tax and estate planning. Some bank loan pricing depends on assets held at the bank, so compare the total cost of the bundled relationship with the cost of keeping the services separate.

How do private banks handle a large single stock position?

Private banks commonly offer securities-based lending against the stock, hedging with collars or prepaid variable forwards through their own trading desks, access to exchange funds, and charitable or estate strategies. An independent advisor can evaluate the same categories of strategies and may compare pricing from more than one counterparty or exchange fund sponsor. Each approach carries tax, liquidity, and counterparty trade-offs that should be reviewed with a CPA and attorney.

Is an independent advisor cheaper than a private bank?

Not necessarily. Compare the all-in cost: the advisory or wealth management fee, underlying fund and product expenses, trading and custody costs, placement or platform fees on alternatives, and loan spreads. Ask each firm to put every layer in writing for your specific portfolio.

A practical framework

Compare the structure, not the brand

  1. List what you need: credit, investments, alternatives, trust services, planning.
  2. Get the all-in cost of each proposal in dollars.
  3. Read each firm's Form ADV or Form CRS for conflicts.
  4. Decide which services belong together and which work better separately.
