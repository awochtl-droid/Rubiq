Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust (ILIT): How It Works and When It Makes Sense

Life insurance is income-tax-free to your heirs, but if you own the policy, the death benefit counts toward your taxable estate. An irrevocable life insurance trust moves the policy outside your estate and controls how the money is used. Here is how it works, what it costs, and who actually needs one.

The short answer

An irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT) owns a life insurance policy on your life and receives the death benefit. Because you don’t own the policy, the proceeds are generally kept out of your taxable estate. In exchange, you give up control: the trust is irrevocable and is run by a trustee.

 You own the policyAn ILIT owns the policy
Income tax on the death benefitGenerally noneGenerally none
Counts toward federal estate taxYesGenerally no
Control of the proceeds after deathPaid outright to beneficiariesManaged under the trust’s terms
Can you change it later?YesVery limited

Federal estate tax exemption: $15 million per person, $30 million per married couple, in 2026. Simplified; trust terms and facts control the result.

How an ILIT works, step by step

  1. An attorney drafts the trust. You sign an irrevocable trust naming a trustee and beneficiaries, and the trust gets its own tax ID.
  2. The trust buys the policy. The trustee applies for insurance on your life, with the trust as owner and beneficiary. Married couples often use a second-to-die (survivorship) policy, because estate tax is usually due at the second death.
  3. You fund the premiums with gifts. You give cash to the trust each year, and the trustee pays the premium.
  4. Beneficiaries get Crummey notices. Each gift gives beneficiaries a short window to withdraw it, which lets the gift qualify for the $19,000-per-beneficiary annual exclusion in 2026. They normally let the window lapse.
  5. At death, the trustee collects the benefit and uses it under the trust’s terms: lending to or buying assets from the estate to provide cash for taxes, supporting a surviving spouse, or holding money for children over time.

The three-year rule

If you move a policy you already own into an ILIT and die within three years, the death benefit is pulled back into your estate. Where health allows, having the trust buy a new policy from the start avoids the problem. For an existing policy, see moving a policy you already own below.

A worked example

A married couple in their late 60s has an estate of $40 million, including a family business and real estate. Their combined federal exemption in 2026 is $30 million, so about $10 million would be taxable at the second death. They want their three children to inherit the business without selling it to pay the tax.

 Couple owns $5M policyILIT owns $5M policy
Taxable estate above the $30M exemption$15,000,000$10,000,000
Federal estate tax at 40%$6,000,000$4,000,000
Cash from the policy available to heirs$5,000,000$5,000,000

Hypothetical and simplified; assumes the 2026 exemption, a flat 40% rate above it, and no state estate tax. The exemption is indexed for inflation in later years.

Owned personally, the policy would add $2 million of estate tax, taking much of its own benefit. Inside the ILIT, the full $5 million is available to provide cash for the $4 million bill, so the business does not have to be sold. With two parents and three children, the couple can give up to $114,000 a year to the trust within the 2026 annual exclusions to cover premiums.

Do you still need an ILIT with a $15 million exemption?

The 2025 tax law set the federal estate tax exemption at $15 million per person for 2026, or $30 million for a married couple, indexed for inflation and with no scheduled sunset. That takes many families out of the estate tax entirely, and some older ILITs set up when the exemption was much lower may no longer be needed for tax reasons. Before letting one go, it is worth checking four things:

  • Growth. Hypothetically, a $12 million estate growing at 6% a year would double in about 12 years; actual growth will vary. The policy is often meant for the estate you will have, not the one you have now.
  • Law changes. “No sunset” means the current law has no end date, not that Congress can’t lower the exemption later.
  • State taxes. Several states, including New York, Massachusetts, and Maryland, have their own estate tax with much lower thresholds. Pennsylvania does not tax life insurance proceeds, but if you live in, or own real estate in, another state, that state’s estate or inheritance tax may apply.
  • Non-tax reasons. Control, creditor protection, blended-family planning, and cash for an illiquid estate still apply at any estate size.

If an existing ILIT no longer fits, an estate attorney may be able to change it through decanting, a trust protector, or state-law modification, and the trustee can review whether the policy itself still makes sense. See our 2026 estate tax exemption guide.

When an ILIT makes sense, and when it doesn’t

Often worth considering

  • Estates near or above the federal exemption, including expected growth
  • Illiquid estates: a business, farm, or real estate
  • Blended families or second marriages
  • Beneficiaries who are young, have special needs, or face creditor or divorce risk

Often unnecessary

  • Estates well below the exemption with no control concerns
  • Term insurance meant only to replace income for a few years
  • Families who want to keep the flexibility to change beneficiaries or cash in the policy

Moving a policy you already own into an ILIT

Buying a new policy inside the trust is the cleanest route. When health or cost makes that impractical, there are two ways to move an existing policy:

 Give the policy to the ILITSell the policy to the ILIT
Three-year ruleApplies: death within three years pulls the benefit back into your estateGenerally avoided, because a sale for full value is not a gift
Gift taxA gift of the policy’s value, which may use annual exclusion or lifetime exemptionNo gift if the trust pays fair value; the trust needs cash, often from an earlier gift
Income tax on the death benefitGenerally noneGenerally none if the trust is treated as wholly owned by you for income tax purposes; otherwise the transfer-for-value rule can make part of the benefit taxable
Main costThe three-year riskA policy valuation and careful drafting

The sale route relies on the trust being treated as wholly owned by you for income tax purposes. Revenue Ruling 2007-13 holds that a transfer to such a trust is treated as a transfer to the insured, so the transfer-for-value rule does not apply. Your attorney may need to add a provision, such as a power to swap assets, to get that status. The sale also needs an independent valuation of the policy.

How an ILIT is taxed

  • The death benefit. Generally free of federal income tax for the trust and its beneficiaries, as with any life insurance paid by reason of death.
  • Income tax while you are alive. Most ILITs are grantor trusts, because trust income can be used to pay premiums on your life. Any income, often little or none, is reported on your own return, and the trust may not need to file its own Form 1041. Your CPA and attorney should confirm how yours is treated.
  • After your death. The trust usually becomes a separate taxpayer. Investment income kept in the trust is taxed at trust rates, which reach the top bracket at a much lower income than for individuals, so distributions and investments should be planned with that in mind.
  • Gift tax. Premium gifts qualify for the annual exclusion when Crummey powers are used correctly. Larger gifts use lifetime exemption and are reported on Form 709.
  • Generation-skipping transfer tax. If grandchildren may benefit, allocating GST exemption ($15 million in 2026) to every premium gift, including annual exclusion gifts, keeps the trust’s GST inclusion ratio at zero, so the death benefit can pass free of GST tax. That is why many ILITs are written to last for several generations where state law allows.

Paying larger premiums

Annual exclusion gifts don’t always cover the premium on a large policy. Common ways to fill the gap:

  • Lifetime exemption gifts. A larger one-time gift to the trust, sometimes enough to prepay premiums for years, using part of your $15 million exemption.
  • Private split-dollar arrangements. You advance the premiums and are repaid from the death benefit or cash value, which can reduce the gift each year. These need careful documentation and an exit plan, and the amount owed back to you stays in your estate.
  • Premium financing. The trust borrows from a lender to pay premiums. It can work for very large policies, but it adds interest-rate, collateral, and policy-performance risk, including having to post more collateral or being unable to repay, that should be stress-tested before you commit.

Choosing the trustee and beneficiaries

  • Trustee. Not you, the insured. Families often choose an adult child, a sibling, a trusted friend, or a professional trustee. The trustee pays premiums, sends Crummey notices, keeps records, and manages the proceeds after death.
  • Your spouse as a beneficiary. Common, and it lets the proceeds support your spouse without being included in their estate later, as long as your spouse does not contribute to the trust (gifts should come from your own funds, not a joint account) and holds only limited powers. Your spouse can sometimes serve as trustee if distributions are limited to health, education, maintenance, and support; your attorney will set that standard.
  • Flexibility. Many newer ILITs name a trust protector who can make limited changes, such as updating administrative terms or moving the trust to another state, without you holding powers that would pull the policy back into your estate.

What happens when the insured dies

  1. The trustee files the death claim with the insurer and deposits the proceeds in the trust account.
  2. If the estate needs cash for taxes or debts, the trustee can lend to the estate or buy assets from it at fair value. The trust can permit, but must not require, the trustee to pay the estate’s taxes or debts; a binding obligation pulls those proceeds back into the estate to that extent.
  3. The trustee invests and distributes the money under the trust’s terms: outright, in stages, or held for life for each beneficiary.
  4. The trust gets its own tax filings going, typically a Form 1041 for each year with $600 or more of gross income.

Common ILIT mistakes

  • Paying premiums directly to the insurer instead of gifting to the trust first.
  • Skipping or backdating Crummey notices.
  • Naming yourself, your estate, or a revocable trust as owner or beneficiary of the policy.
  • Keeping powers over the policy, such as the right to change beneficiaries or borrow against it.
  • Never reviewing the policy, so a universal life policy quietly heads toward lapse.

Costs and ongoing administration

  • Legal fees to draft the trust, plus any later amendments allowed under state law.
  • Annual administration: a trust bank account, Crummey notices for each gift, premium payments, and records the trustee should keep.
  • Gift tax returns (Form 709) when gifts exceed the annual exclusion or when you allocate generation-skipping transfer tax exemption to the trust.
  • The insurance itself. Premiums depend on age, health, policy type, and guarantees. Compare guaranteed and non-guaranteed illustrations, not just the premium.

An ILIT is only as good as its administration. Missed Crummey notices or premiums paid directly by the insured can weaken the plan.

Pennsylvania families

Life insurance proceeds are generally exempt from Pennsylvania inheritance tax even without a trust. Because policies already escape the state tax, the reasons to use an ILIT in Pennsylvania are the federal estate tax, control of the proceeds, and protecting beneficiaries. Proceeds can also give heirs the cash to pay Pennsylvania inheritance tax on other assets within three months and capture the 5% discount.

Frequently asked questions

What is an irrevocable life insurance trust (ILIT)?

An ILIT is a trust that owns a life insurance policy on your life and is named as its beneficiary. Because you do not own the policy, the death benefit is generally not included in your taxable estate for federal estate tax purposes. The trustee collects the proceeds at your death and manages or distributes them under the trust’s terms, for example to pay estate taxes, provide for a spouse, or hold money for children.

How are premiums paid in an ILIT?

You typically give cash to the trust and the trustee pays the premium. To make those gifts qualify for the annual gift tax exclusion, $19,000 per beneficiary in 2026, the trust usually gives beneficiaries a temporary right to withdraw each gift, known as a Crummey power. Beneficiaries receive notice and generally let the right lapse so the trustee can pay the premium.

What is the three-year rule for life insurance trusts?

If you transfer a policy you already own into an ILIT and die within three years, the death benefit is pulled back into your taxable estate. Having the trust apply for and buy a new policy from the start avoids the three-year rule, although health and insurability affect whether that is practical.

Do I need an ILIT if my estate is below the federal exemption?

Often not for tax reasons. The federal estate tax exemption is $15 million per person in 2026, or $30 million for a married couple. Families below that may still use an ILIT to control how proceeds are used, protect them from a beneficiary’s creditors or divorce, provide for a blended family or a beneficiary with special needs, or plan for future growth in the estate.

Can I be the trustee of my own ILIT?

Generally no. If you, the insured, have control over the policy, the death benefit can be included in your estate. Families usually name an adult child, another relative, a trusted friend, or a professional trustee. The trust should be drafted by an estate planning attorney.

Is life insurance subject to Pennsylvania inheritance tax?

No. Life insurance proceeds paid because of the insured’s death are generally exempt from Pennsylvania inheritance tax whether or not they are held in a trust. For Pennsylvania families, an ILIT is mainly about the federal estate tax, control of the proceeds, and protecting beneficiaries.

Are irrevocable life insurance trusts taxable?

The death benefit is generally free of federal income tax and, if the trust is set up correctly, is kept out of your taxable estate. While you are alive, most ILITs are grantor trusts, so any trust income is reported on your own return. After your death, the trust usually becomes a separate taxpayer and pays tax on any investment income it keeps, at trust tax rates.

Can I move a life insurance policy I already own into an ILIT?

Yes. You can give it to the trust, but if you die within three years of the gift the death benefit is included in your estate. Selling the policy to an ILIT that is treated as wholly owned by you for income tax purposes generally avoids the three-year rule and the transfer-for-value problem, but it needs an independent valuation and an estate attorney.

Can my spouse be the beneficiary of my ILIT?

Yes. Many ILITs provide for a surviving spouse for life, then pass the rest to children. Holding the proceeds in trust rather than paying them outright can keep them out of your spouse’s estate as well, provided your spouse does not contribute to the trust and holds only limited powers. Your spouse can sometimes serve as trustee if distributions are limited to health, education, maintenance, and support.

What happens to an ILIT when the insured dies?

The trustee collects the death benefit, provides cash to the estate if needed by lending to it or buying assets from it, and then manages or distributes the money under the trust’s terms. The trust typically files its own income tax return for each year with $600 or more of gross income.

Before you set up an ILIT

ILIT checklist

  1. Estimate your estate tax exposure, including expected growth.
  2. Decide what the proceeds should do: pay taxes, support a spouse, or hold for children.
  3. Choose a trustee other than yourself.
  4. Have an estate attorney draft the trust before the policy is bought.
  5. Have the trust apply for the policy, rather than transferring an existing one, where possible.
  6. Set up a trust account and a Crummey notice routine for every gift.
  7. Decide whether to allocate GST exemption if grandchildren may benefit.
  8. Review the policy’s performance with the trustee every few years.
