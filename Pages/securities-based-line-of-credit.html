SBLOC Rates: How a Securities-Based Line of Credit Works

Borrowing against your portfolio can fund a purchase, a tax bill, or a bridge loan without selling appreciated stock. Here is how the rate is set, what the published rates are today, and the risks that come with it.

A securities-based line of credit (SBLOC) is a revolving loan secured by a taxable investment account. Your rate is a benchmark, usually SOFR, plus a spread that gets smaller as the line gets bigger. That spread is the part worth negotiating, and it varies more between lenders than most borrowers realize.

How SBLOC rates are set

Nearly every securities-based line floats. The rate has two parts:

  • The index: usually the Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR), which the New York Fed publishes every business day. It moves with Federal Reserve policy, so your rate changes when SOFR does. SOFR was 3.95% on November 2, 2026 (New York Fed).
  • The spread: a fixed margin the lender adds on top. It is set by the size of your line, and sometimes by how much else you keep with the firm.

You pay interest only on what you draw, not on the full line, and most lenders require only monthly interest payments.

How much you can borrow

Lenders lend a percentage of each holding’s value, called the loan value or advance rate. According to FINRA, it is typically 50% to 95% of the account value depending on what you own: broadly diversified stocks and funds sit at the lower end, high-grade bonds and Treasuries at the higher end. Concentrated positions, volatile stocks, and some funds may get less or nothing. Retirement accounts cannot be pledged.

The risks to understand first

  • Maintenance calls. If your holdings fall and the collateral no longer supports the loan, you must add assets or pay down the balance, usually within two or three days.
  • Forced sales. If you cannot meet a call, the lender can sell your securities without notice, at a bad time and possibly with a tax bill.
  • Demand loan. The lender can generally call the loan or change collateral rules at any time.
  • Rising rates. A floating rate means your cost goes up when SOFR does.
  • Harder to move. Pledged assets usually cannot transfer to another firm until the loan is repaid or refinanced.

Source: FINRA: Securities-Backed Lines of Credit. A conservative rule many planners use is to draw well below the maximum so a market drop does not trigger a call.

Taxes: borrowing instead of selling

Borrowing does not trigger capital gains tax, which is the main reason families with low-basis stock use these lines instead of selling. Whether the interest is deductible depends on how the money is used: interest on personal spending generally is not, while interest traced to an investment or a business may be, subject to limits. Some families hold appreciated assets for life and borrow against them, so heirs receive a step-up in basis. That approach, sometimes called “buy, borrow, die,” depends on current tax law and on carrying the loan through every market. Talk to your CPA before you borrow.

Wirehouse and private bank lending vs. an independent advisor

Lending is a big reason families keep assets at a wirehouse or private bank. The firm lends from its own balance sheet, and the rate can improve as you move more assets there. That can be a good trade, or it can be offset by higher fees elsewhere in the relationship. An independent advisor does not lend, but can compare your current line with custodian lines, such as those at Schwab and Fidelity, and with other lenders. Questions to ask any lender

  • What is my spread over SOFR, and what would lower it?
  • What advance rate applies to each of my holdings?
  • At what account value would I get a maintenance call, and how long would I have to meet it?
  • Can you change collateral rules or call the loan, and with what notice?
  • Does my advisor or the firm earn anything from this loan?
  • What happens to the line if I move my accounts?

Frequently asked questions

What is an SBLOC?

A securities-based line of credit (SBLOC) is a revolving loan secured by the stocks, bonds, and funds in a taxable investment account. You can borrow against the portfolio without selling it, pay interest only on what you draw, and repay on your own schedule. The lender can require more collateral or sell holdings if the account value falls.

What are current SBLOC rates?

Most SBLOCs float at SOFR plus a spread that shrinks as the line gets larger. As of November 2, 2026, with SOFR at 3.95%, Schwab’s Pledged Asset Line ranged from 8.35% for the smallest lines to 6.35% for $2.5 million and up, before relationship discounts, and Fidelity’s line of credit spreads ranged from SOFR + 3.10% to SOFR + 1.90%. Large relationships can often negotiate lower spreads.

What is the difference between an SBLOC and a margin loan?

Both are secured by your investments. A margin loan can be used to buy more securities, while an SBLOC is a non-purpose loan that cannot. SBLOCs are usually set up in a separate pledged account and are often priced lower than standard margin rates for larger balances.

Can I use an SBLOC to buy stocks?

No. SBLOC proceeds cannot be used to buy or carry securities. They can be used for almost anything else, such as real estate, a tax bill, a business need, or bridge financing.

Is SBLOC interest tax-deductible?

It depends on how the money is used. Interest on money used for personal purposes is generally not deductible. Interest traced to an investment, such as a real estate purchase held for investment, or to a business may be deductible under the interest tracing rules, subject to limits. Confirm with your CPA before you borrow.

What happens to an SBLOC if the market falls?

If the value of your collateral drops below what the loan requires, you will get a maintenance call to add assets or pay down the loan, usually within a few days. If you cannot, the lender can sell your holdings without notice, which may also trigger capital gains tax.

What is the buy, borrow, die strategy?

It describes borrowing against appreciated assets instead of selling them, then holding the assets until death so heirs receive a step-up in cost basis. It can defer or avoid capital gains tax, but the interest compounds, the loan carries market risk, and the result depends on tax law that can change.

Before you borrow

Four numbers to know

  1. Your spread over SOFR, in writing.
  2. The advance rate on each holding.
  3. The account value that would trigger a call.
  4. The tax you would owe if you sold instead.
